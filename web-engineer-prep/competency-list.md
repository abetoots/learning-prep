# What I can do

This is the most important file here. Write it first, before you study anything.

**The rule:** every line starts with **"I can…"** and describes something you *do*, not something you *know about*.

> ❌ "Learn JavaScript arrays"
> ✅ "I can write a loop that prints the numbers 1 to 10, from memory, without looking anything up."

**Make each one small enough that you could succeed at it today or tomorrow.** That is deliberate, not a lowered bar. Early wins are what keep you going, and nobody gives you credit for a target you set and never hit.

---

## Why there is a Proof column

Anyone can now ask an AI for a finished website and get one back in a couple of minutes. So a finished website doesn't show that *you* can build it — and the people who hire know that.

What still counts is anything that shows **the work happening**: you explaining a choice, you finding a mistake, you building something again without help.

**You don't have to invent the proof.** Pick one off this list. That's all the column needs.

| Pick one | What you actually do |
|---|---|
| **Build it again blind** | Close everything and build the same thing from an empty file. If you can do that, you know it |
| **Say it out loud** | Record yourself for 2 minutes, no notes, explaining how it works and why you did it that way |
| **Show the mistake** | Write down what broke, what you thought was happening, and what was really happening |
| **Get it reviewed** | Send it to Person 1, and write down what they told you to change |
| **Prove it stays fixed** | Once you know what a test is — around week 2 or 3, not now — write one that fails before your fix and passes after |

If you can't pick one, the line it belongs to is probably still a *topic* rather than something you can do. Make it smaller and try again.

## The list

**This file is yours to write in.** Person 1 reads it, and on Saturday you set the coming week's lines together. But they don't write in it — you do.

The reason: these have to be targets *you* can name. Once someone else's lines start appearing, you lose track of which ones are yours, and naming your own is most of the point.

**On day one, read your first list out to Person 1 before you start.** Writing a good "I can…" line is a skill, and it's one of the things you're here to learn, so the first attempt is worth ten minutes of someone checking it. They say what they'd change; **you still type every word.** That's true on day one and every day after.

*Starting five, drafted with Person 1 on day one — more than the 2–3 you'll set on a normal Saturday, because these first ones are small enough that several can land in a day. Confirm or change them at your first Saturday; they're a starting point, not a verdict.*

| # | I can… | Proof | Date done |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | I can explain out loud what happens between typing a web address and the page appearing, in under 2 minutes. | Say it out loud | |
| 2 | I can write an HTML page with a heading, a paragraph and a link, from a blank editor, without looking it up. | Build it again blind | |
| 3 | I can say what makes a tag "semantic", and give two cases where I'd pick one over a plain `div`. | Say it out loud | |
| 4 | I can build a page as a **real file on my own computer**, in a real editor, opened in my own browser — not only inside the freeCodeCamp window. | Get it reviewed | |
| 5 | I can change the colour or size of text on a page with CSS, and say what went wrong when it doesn't work. | Show the mistake | |

**Two of these are worth pointing out.**

**#1 and #3 are explaining, not building.** They're here on purpose. If every line is something you type, you end up able to produce code you can't account for — and "why did you do it that way?" is the question every interview and every code review is actually made of.

**#4 is not in your course**, and that's exactly why it's there. freeCodeCamp runs in the browser, which is great for getting started and quietly skips something real: making a file, saving it somewhere sensible, and opening it yourself. Person 1 spotted that gap and will show you. Expect a few more like it.

### A line is done when the proof exists — not when you feel ready

That's the whole rule. Not "I think I've got this." You did the thing in the Proof column, and it worked. Put the date in.

Feeling ready is exactly what misleads people. Something you've just watched feels familiar, and familiar feels like knowing it. The proof is what tells you the difference.

### Which lines you work on this week

**Saturday picks the week's 2–3 lines.** You and Person 1 choose them together from what your course covers next. Those are the ones you work on Monday to Friday, and you don't have to decide what's next on a Tuesday night — that's already settled.

**You can still add lines any time.** Something occurs to you mid-week, write it down. Mark it with a `?` and leave it alone until Saturday. It isn't this week's work; it's a note to raise. Anything with a `?` gets picked up, split, or dropped on Saturday.

That's the split: **the list is yours, the week's selection is agreed.** Nobody is editing over anybody.

### When you touch this file

| When | What you do |
|---|---|
| **End of every session** | Tick off anything whose proof you produced today. Add a `?` line if something came up |
| **Friday** | Read through it. Collect what you want to raise tomorrow — the `?` lines, anything undone all week, anything you now think was a bad idea. **You're gathering, not deciding** |
| **Saturday, with Person 1** | They read the list, you go through your Friday collection, and you set next week's 2–3 lines |

**Friday is for gathering, not judging — and that's deliberate.** You are not asked to work out on your own whether a line is a real ability or just a topic wearing one. Telling those apart is the expertise you're here to build. If you could already do it reliably, you wouldn't have written the topic. So that call happens Saturday, with someone who can already make it.

### If a line sat undone all week

Bring it to Saturday and **name which of these it was**, before deciding you didn't try hard enough. **Work down the list in order** — the first one is the most common, and if you skip past it you'll end up fixing something that wasn't broken:

| Why it stalled | What happens |
|---|---|
| **It never actually got worked on** — the sessions went elsewhere | Make next week's target smaller, so it gets reached |
| **Something has to come first** | Add the missing piece as its own line, ahead of it |
| **Too big** | Split it into two smaller lines |

Being stuck is information about the line, not a verdict on you. Almost every stalled line is one of these three, and all three have a fix.
